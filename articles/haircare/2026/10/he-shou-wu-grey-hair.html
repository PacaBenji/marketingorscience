He Shou Wu, sold in the West as fo-ti, is the dried root of Polygonum multiflorum (also classified as Reynoutria multiflora). Its name is usually translated along the lines of “black-haired Mr. He,” after a legendary figure said to have regained his youth and dark hair by eating the root. That story is still doing the marketing: He Shou Wu capsules, teas, and tonics are sold to reverse greying, thicken hair, and restore vitality.

Grey hair is one of the few hair concerns with no approved treatment, which makes it fertile ground for supplements. The question is whether He Shou Wu has any human evidence behind it, and what the trade-off is.

The Claim

“A time-tested herbal tonic that restores your hair’s natural color and strength from within — nature’s answer to grey hair.”

(Composite representative claim drawn from supplement marketing.)

Why Hair Turns Grey

Hair gets its color from melanocytes in each follicle, which are replenished by a pool of melanocyte stem cells. With age, that pool is depleted and oxidative stress, including hydrogen peroxide build-up, impairs pigment production. A 2018 review of premature greying also links it to vitamin B12 deficiency, low copper, ferritin, or vitamin D, and thyroid disease. Those causes are worth testing for, because correcting a real deficiency is the one situation where some repigmentation is plausible.

Natural reversal does happen, but rarely. A 2021 study in eLife mapped pigment along 397 individual hairs and found repigmentation in 10 people aged 9 to 39. The authors concluded that reversal is limited to isolated follicles sitting just above a greying threshold. Once a follicle’s stem cells are gone, no supplement is known to bring them back. That is a useful backdrop for any product that promises to “restore” color.

What the Hair Studies Actually Show

We found no peer-reviewed, controlled human trials showing that He Shou Wu reverses grey hair or improves hair growth. The research behind the claims is in animals and cells:

  • Han et al. (2015) induced greying in mice with hydrogen peroxide and reported 37% more hair melanin with raw extract and 28% with processed extract. Hair samples were pooled within each group, so each result effectively represents a single measurement.
  • Li et al. (2015) found that oral extract sped regrowth over shaved skin in mice (8 per group), although final hair length was similar across groups.
  • Cell studies, such as work in B16F10 melanoma cells, show compounds from the root can increase melanin production in a dish.

These studies justify further research, not consumer claims. Mouse hair cycles and induced greying models differ from human age-related greying, and a melanin increase in cultured cells says nothing about whether an oral dose reaches human follicles.

The Liver Problem

The safety evidence is much stronger than the efficacy evidence. A 2015 systematic review collected 450 published cases of liver injury associated with Polygonum multiflorum. In the case reports where a reason for use was recorded, about 60% of people were taking it for grey hair or hair loss. The median time to injury was 30 days. The review recorded several deaths and two liver transplants, and found that injury occurred with both raw and processed root.

  • A Korean case series described 25 people with acute hepatitis after taking the herb.
  • A 2019 review reported more than 10,000 related complaints in China’s adverse-reaction system. China capped doses in health foods in 2014 and required a liver warning, and regulators in the UK, Canada, and Australia have also issued warnings.
  • Suspected culprits include anthraquinones such as emodin and the stilbene glucoside TSG. The injury appears to be largely idiosyncratic, and a 2019 genetic study identified the HLA-B*35:01 allele as a potential risk marker. That means it is hard to predict who will be affected, and “low dose” is not a reliable safeguard.

The U.S. National Institutes of Health’s LiverTox database maintains an entry on the herb. Anyone taking He Shou Wu who develops fatigue, nausea, dark urine, or yellowing skin or eyes should stop and see a doctor.

The Regulatory Picture

In the U.S., He Shou Wu is sold as a dietary supplement, so it does not need FDA approval before sale. In April 2026, the FDA issued a warning letter to Nature’s Elements, Inc., a seller of He Shou Wu products. The letter cited a cholesterol-lowering claim for He Shou Wu and a hair-loss claim for a separate “DHT Blocker” product, and it described manufacturing gaps, including no finished-product specifications or batch records for the He Shou Wu product. The letter did not address grey-hair claims, but it illustrates how loosely these products can be made and marketed.

Better Options for Grey Hair

There is no proven way to reverse genetically driven greying. If greying is premature or sudden, it is worth checking B12, iron, vitamin D, and thyroid function with a doctor. Otherwise, hair dye remains the effective option. For hair thinning, our reviews of hair growth supplements and biotin cover the better-studied choices.

Verdict: Claim Unsupported

There is no controlled human evidence that He Shou Wu reverses grey hair or regrows hair; the support comes from a legend, a handful of mouse studies, and cell experiments. Meanwhile, hundreds of published cases link it to liver injury, many in people taking it for their hair. The risk is documented; the benefit is not.
