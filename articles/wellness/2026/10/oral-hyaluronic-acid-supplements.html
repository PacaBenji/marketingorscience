Hyaluronic acid (HA) is one of skincare’s most familiar ingredients. As a serum, it is a humectant that pulls water into the outer layers of the skin, and we covered that use in our topical hyaluronic acid review. The newer pitch is ingestible: capsules, gummies, and powders sold as a way to “hydrate your skin from the inside out.”

That sounds intuitive. HA is a major component of the skin’s own extracellular matrix, and levels decline with age. But swallowing a molecule is not the same as delivering it to the dermis, and most of the human evidence comes from a small number of industry-linked trials.

The Claim

“Oral hyaluronic acid replenishes the HA your skin loses with age, for deep hydration and fewer wrinkles — like drinking your moisturizer.”

(Composite representative claim drawn from supplement marketing.)

The Absorption Problem

Supplement-grade HA is a large polysaccharide, often hundreds of thousands of daltons. It is far too big to be absorbed intact like a vitamin. Lab studies suggest it largely survives stomach acid and small-intestine digestion, then gets broken down by gut bacteria in the large intestine, where smaller fragments are absorbed. A 2023 study concluded that molecular weight and the gut microbiota together determine how much is bioavailable.

The animal tracer studies point in different directions. In the most-cited radiolabeled study in rats and dogs, most of the radioactivity ended up in feces, though some label was detected in tissues including skin. A separate preclinical study found no significant absorption into the bloodstream. Tracking a radioactive label also does not show whether intact HA arrived, or just fragments and breakdown products.

The leading alternative explanation is indirect. A 2010 mouse study found that high-molecular-weight HA interacts with TLR4 receptors in the gut lining and shifts immune signaling. That is a plausible mechanism, but it is also a very different story from “replenishing the HA in your skin.”

What the Human Trials Show

Most trials use 120–240 mg per day for 6 to 12 weeks, with skin hydration measured by corneometer and wrinkles assessed by imaging.

  • Kawada et al. (2015): 61 adults with dry skin took 120 mg/day of HA (800 kDa or 300 kDa) or placebo for 6 weeks. Both HA groups showed greater improvement in skin moisture than placebo.
  • Oe et al. (2017): 60 participants were randomized to 120 mg/day HA or placebo for 12 weeks; 50 were analyzed. The 300 kDa group had lower wrinkle volume than placebo at week 8 (p=0.046), but no outcome was significant at week 12. The placebo group started with lower wrinkle scores. The study was funded by Kewpie, which makes the HA ingredient used, and six of seven authors were company employees.
  • Hsu et al. (2021): 40 participants took 120 mg/day HA or placebo for 12 weeks. Facial hydration and water loss improved versus placebo. Absolute wrinkle scores did not differ, only percentage change. Many outcomes were tested with no correction for multiple comparisons. Kewpie funded the study, and three authors were employees.
  • Gao et al. (2023): about 129 women took 100 or 200 mg/day or placebo for 12 weeks. Results were reported mostly as within-group changes from baseline rather than direct comparisons with placebo. The paper declared no funding, but four of seven authors were employees of Bloomage, an HA manufacturer that supplied the product.

A 2025 meta-analysis in the Journal of Drugs in Dermatology pooled seven placebo-controlled RCTs. It reported significant improvements in hydration, elasticity, and wrinkle depth, but not in firmness, wrinkle volume, or transepidermal water loss. The authors themselves noted small sample sizes and heterogeneity between trials. An earlier 2022 meta-analysis of moisturizing supplements in general, including collagen, ceramides, and HA, also reported a benefit on skin hydration.

Reading the Evidence Carefully

A meta-analysis is only as strong as the studies it combines. Here the underlying trials are small, short, often run in a single country, and frequently designed, funded, or staffed by companies that sell the ingredient. Industry funding does not make a result false, but it raises the bar for independent replication, and that replication is largely missing.

The effects themselves are modest instrument readings: a few units on a corneometer, or a change in imaging-based wrinkle scores. None of the trials compared oral HA with simply using a good moisturizer, which is cheap, works on contact, and has a well-understood mechanism. It is also unclear whether the benefit comes from HA reaching the skin at all, rather than from gut-mediated signaling.

As with collagen supplements, oral HA is sold as a dietary supplement in the U.S., which means products do not need FDA approval or proof of efficacy before going on sale, and doses and molecular weights vary between brands.

Is It Worth Taking?

Oral HA appears to be well tolerated in these trials, and the hydration signal is real enough that it is not pure marketing. But it is a small effect, built on industry-linked studies, with a mechanism that is still being worked out. If dry skin is the goal, a topical humectant such as HA or glycerin under a moisturizer is the better-supported first step. Anyone who tries a supplement should give it the same 8–12 weeks used in trials, and judge it on whether their skin actually changes.

Verdict: Partially Supported

Small randomized trials and a 2025 meta-analysis suggest oral hyaluronic acid can modestly improve skin hydration, with weaker and less consistent effects on wrinkles. The research is largely industry-linked, the HA is mostly broken down before absorption, and “drinking your moisturizer” overstates what has been shown.
